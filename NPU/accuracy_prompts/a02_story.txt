The lighthouse keeper had not spoken to another person for eleven days when the boat appeared. It came in the grey hour before dawn, low in the water, its single sail patched in three colours. Mara watched it from the gallery with the brass telescope her father had left her, and for a long time she did not move.

The island was small enough to walk around in an hour. There was the tower, the keeper's cottage with its slate roof, a vegetable garden that the wind tried to take every winter, and a stone jetty that the supply ship used once a month. The supply ship was not due for another nineteen days. Nobody else ever came.

She went down the spiral stairs, counting them as she always did, one hundred and twelve, and pulled on her oilskin at the door. The wind smelled of rain. By the time she reached the jetty the boat was close enough for her to see a figure at the tiller, wrapped in a coat too large for it, and a second shape lying very still in the bottom of the hull.

"Throw me a line," the figure called. It was a girl's voice, younger than Mara had expected, and it was shaking.

Mara caught the rope on the second try and made it fast to the iron ring. Up close the girl could not have been more than fifteen. Her hands were raw and her lips were cracked from salt. She did not wait to be asked anything.

"My brother is sick," she said. "We have been at sea for four days. The chart said there was a light here, and a keeper, and I thought a keeper would know what to do."

Mara looked at the boy in the bottom of the boat. His face was pale and his breathing was shallow and quick. She had a first aid cabinet, a radio that worked when the weather allowed it, and a book of instructions that had been written for problems like broken lights and frozen pipes.
