The spread of the printing press in fifteenth century Europe is often told as the story of one inventor, but it is better understood as the meeting of several older technologies with a new kind of business. Paper had been made in Europe for two centuries, after the craft arrived from the Islamic world through Spain and Italy. Screw presses were already used for pressing grapes and olives. Metalworkers knew how to cast small objects in moulds. What changed in the middle of the century was the combination of these skills with movable metal type, cast in large numbers from reusable moulds, and with an ink thick enough to cling to metal.

The first printers were entrepreneurs who borrowed heavily. A press, a stock of type and a supply of paper were expensive, and a printed book earned nothing until the whole edition had been sold. Many early print shops failed. Those that survived found reliable markets: religious texts, grammars for schools, legal documents, calendars, and short pamphlets that could be sold quickly. Within fifty years there were presses in more than two hundred towns, and millions of printed books were in circulation.

The effects were slow and uneven. Printed books were cheaper than manuscripts, but still beyond the means of most people, and literacy rose only gradually. Yet printing made texts stable in a way that copying by hand never had. Every copy of an edition was the same, so a scholar in one city could refer to a page and line that a scholar in another city could find. Errors could be listed and corrected in the next edition. Maps, tables and diagrams could be reproduced without the distortions that crept in when each copy was drawn by hand.

Printing also changed the speed at which arguments travelled. A pamphlet could be written, printed and carried to another country in a matter of weeks, and replies could follow just as quickly. Governments and churches soon tried to control what was printed, with licences and lists of forbidden books, but the number of presses and the ease of moving them across borders made complete control impossible.
